Wordleaf

Write with your voice.

Writing should be free and accessible.

Wordleaf is a free writing program built for people who like to talk through their work. You can dictate a paragraph, make a heading, move a picture, change the page layout, sort notes into an outline, or clean up the document without stopping to hunt through menus.

You can talk to it normally. It can tell when you are writing, quoting someone, thinking through an idea, or asking it to change the document. You can still type whenever you want, but the whole program is designed so your voice can do most of the work.

Speak or type

Dictate full passages, dialogue, notes, or rough ideas. Wordleaf can clean up obvious speech-to-text mess, add punctuation, keep quoted speech together, and remember spellings or terms you want it to preserve.

Format with your voice

Tell it to make a heading, start a list, add a page break, change margins or spacing, add page numbers, make columns, build a table of contents, move text, or change how part of the page is laid out.

Work with pictures and visuals

Add and place pictures, move them around the document, and work with tables, figures, charts, diagrams, timelines, equations, captions, references, and other visual or technical material.

Organize your outline

Bring in your scenes, notes, chapters, or ideas and sort them into structures such as the Hero’s Journey, Story Circle, Save the Cat, or a 27-point structure. Wordleaf can show what fits where, what is missing, and what may work better in a different order.

Essays, research, and nonfiction

Organize essays, reports, research papers, theses, literature reviews, citations, references, tables, and figures. It also has guidance for APA, MLA, Chicago/Turabian, IEEE, AMA, and common report formats.

Bring it in, finish it, export it

Import Word files, Google Docs exports, plain text, or Markdown. Choose a page format, preview the finished pages as you work, export a PDF, or save the full editable project so you can reopen it later.

Writing with your voice
Can I use my voice for formatting, not just dictation?

Yes. Wordleaf is designed so voice can be used for document instructions as well as writing. You can ask for headings, lists, tables, captions, page breaks, columns, margins, headers, footers, page numbers, fonts, spacing, alignment, table-of-contents changes, references, image placement, and other document changes. The dictation layer uses context to distinguish ordinary writing from a command.

Can Wordleaf read my writing aloud?

Yes. Wordleaf includes local reading voices with multiple American and British voice choices and adjustable reading speed, so you can listen back to the document while revising it.

What is the Word Bank and what are Writing Rules?

The Word Bank stores spellings and preferred terms you want Wordleaf to preserve. Writing Rules are instructions you explicitly tell Wordleaf to remember for the project. They are used as author preferences when Wordleaf interprets dictation or changes the document.

Can Wordleaf help with spelling, punctuation, and messy voice transcription?

Wordleaf’s dictation interpreter can clean speech-recognition artifacts, punctuation, sentence boundaries, dialogue punctuation, and obvious transcription mess while preserving your meaning, names, wording, and preferred spellings. That cleanup is meant to fix the transcription, not turn your sentence into a different piece of writing.

Story and fiction structures
What fiction and story structures does Wordleaf know?

Wordleaf can help organize your existing material with common structures including three-act structure, five-act structure, Freytag’s Pyramid, the Hero’s Journey, seven-point story structure, 27-point or 27-chapter planning, Story Circle, Story Spine, Save the Cat beat-sheet concepts, Kishōtenketsu, scene-sequel organization, the Fichtean Curve, the Snowflake Method, in medias res, frame narrative, nested narrative, parallel narrative, nonlinear narrative, episodic structure, circular structure, quest structure, mystery/reveal structure, romance arcs, tragedy arcs, character arcs, and coming-of-age arcs. These are references for sorting what you already have. If a framework has an empty slot, Wordleaf leaves it open for you instead of filling it with a new plot point.

What are the three-act and five-act structures?

Three-act structure: Act I establishes the situation and disruption; Act II develops complications, pressure, and a midpoint change; Act III brings the final confrontation and resolution. Five-act structure: exposition or setup; rising action; climax or major turning point; falling action or consequences; resolution or denouement. Wordleaf can map your scenes and notes to these parts and show what is still undecided.

What are the stages of the Hero’s Journey?

A common twelve-stage version uses: Ordinary World; Call to Adventure; Refusal of the Call; Meeting the Mentor; Crossing the First Threshold; Tests, Allies, and Enemies; Approach to the Inmost Cave; Ordeal; Reward; Road Back; Resurrection; and Return with the Elixir. Wordleaf can compare your existing scenes, events, and character changes with those stages. If a stage is empty, it points out the gap rather than inventing the event.

What is the seven-point story structure?

The common seven points are: Hook; Plot Turn 1; Pinch Point 1; Midpoint; Pinch Point 2; Plot Turn 2; and Resolution. Wordleaf can place your existing scenes or notes against those points and help you test the order without filling an empty point with a new plot beat.

What is the 27-point or 27-chapter story structure?

There is more than one 27-point variant, so Wordleaf does not pretend there is one universal set of named beats. By default it treats the method as a flexible three-act planning grid with nine blocks and three beats per block. If you are following a particular 27-point method, you can give Wordleaf that version and it will organize your existing material against it. Empty beats stay empty until you decide what belongs there.

What are the eight Story Circle steps?

The Story Circle is commonly summarized as: You; Need; Go; Search; Find; Take; Return; Change. Wordleaf can map your existing material to those eight steps and show where scenes or notes already fit.

What is the Story Spine?

A common Story Spine uses: Once upon a time; Every day; Until one day; Because of that; Because of that; Because of that; Until finally; Ever since then. The repeated “Because of that” section can expand as needed. Wordleaf can use the pattern to organize your own events without creating the missing events for you.

What are the Save the Cat beats?

The standard fifteen beat-sheet labels are: Opening Image; Theme Stated; Setup; Catalyst; Debate; Break into Two; B Story; Fun and Games; Midpoint; Bad Guys Close In; All Is Lost; Dark Night of the Soul; Break into Three; Finale; and Final Image. Wordleaf can help place material you already wrote or planned into those beats, but it does not automatically generate a Catalyst, Midpoint, Finale, or other missing beat.

What is Kishōtenketsu?

Kishōtenketsu uses four broad parts: Ki, introduction; Shō, development; Ten, twist or change; and Ketsu, reconciliation or conclusion. Wordleaf can help organize material this way without forcing a conflict-centered Western plot structure onto it.

What is scene-sequel structure?

A common scene-sequel model uses a scene of Goal, Conflict, and Disaster followed by a sequel of Reaction, Dilemma, and Decision. Wordleaf can help label and order material you already have using those functions without inventing the character’s goal, disaster, dilemma, or decision.

What is the Snowflake Method and can Wordleaf help with it?

The Snowflake Method is a planning method rather than one fixed plot beat sheet. It progressively expands a short premise into a synopsis, character material, scene planning, and increasingly detailed draft preparation. Wordleaf can help organize and expand the material you provide through those stages, but it should not choose the premise, characters, conflicts, or missing story decisions for you.

What nonfiction book structures can Wordleaf help organize?

Wordleaf can help organize chronological, thematic, topical, problem-solution, question-and-answer, how-to or step-by-step, reference/manual, case-study, memoir, biography, braided narrative, essay-collection, and progressive chapter structures. It works with the facts, experiences, examples, and chapter material you provide instead of manufacturing missing expertise.

Can Wordleaf help me write a fiction book?

Yes. You can dictate scenes, chapters, dialogue, notes, character information, and story ideas, then ask Wordleaf to help organize what you already have. It can help map your material to structures such as three-act structure, five-act structure, Freytag’s Pyramid, the Hero’s Journey, the 27-point story structure, seven-point story structure, Story Circle, Story Spine, Fichtean Curve, quest structure, episodic structure, circular structure, nonlinear structure, parallel narrative, frame narrative, mystery/reveal structure, romance arcs, tragedy arcs, coming-of-age arcs, and scene-sequel organization. It can show where your existing scenes fit and where there are empty beats. It can show you the empty beats and help you think them through without turning them into somebody else’s story.

Can Wordleaf help with the Hero’s Journey?

It can help place those ideas against stages such as the ordinary world, call to adventure, refusal, mentor, threshold, tests, ordeal, reward, return, and transformation. If a stage has no material, Wordleaf can point that out or ask you a question. It does not invent the missing event for you.

Can Wordleaf help with a 27-point story structure?

Yes. Wordleaf can help sort your existing ideas across the 27-point framework, identify which points already have scenes or notes attached, and show which points are still empty. It can help you move scenes or ideas into a different order. Empty points remain empty until you decide what belongs there.

What other fiction story structures can it help organize?

Can Wordleaf help with Save the Cat, Story Circle, Freytag’s Pyramid, or the Snowflake Method?

Yes, as organizational references. Wordleaf can help compare your existing notes and scenes with structures or planning methods such as Save the Cat beat-sheet concepts, Story Circle, Freytag’s Pyramid, the Snowflake Method, three-act structure, five-act structure, seven-point structure, eight-sequence structure, the Hero’s Journey, 27-point structure, Story Spine, Fichtean Curve, Kishōtenketsu, scene-sequel organization, and other common narrative frameworks. Yes. Wordleaf can help organize your own scenes, clues, reveals, relationship beats, worldbuilding notes, character arcs, turning points, chapter order, and existing plot material. What is the usual structure of an IMRaD research paper?

IMRaD means Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Depending on the assignment or journal, it can also include a title, abstract, keywords, references, acknowledgments, tables, figures, appendices, or supplementary sections. Wordleaf can organize information you provide into those sections but does not manufacture a method, result, or dataset.

How can Wordleaf organize a literature review?

It can help define the section structure around the scope or research question, then organize supplied literature thematically, methodologically, chronologically, theoretically, or with a mixed approach. It can help group agreements, disagreements, patterns, and gaps that are already present in the material you provide. It does not invent papers or claim that a gap exists without evidence in the supplied material.

How can Wordleaf organize a thesis or dissertation?

A common structure includes front matter; introduction; literature review or background; methods; results or findings; discussion; conclusion; references or bibliography; and appendices. Requirements vary by institution and discipline, so Wordleaf can adapt the organization to the rules you provide rather than assuming one universal thesis format.

Imports, projects, backups, and file types
Can I make a book in Wordleaf instead of Microsoft Word?

Yes. Wordleaf is intended for book-length projects and combines dictation, organization, document formatting, images, page layout, live PDF preview, project saving, and PDF export. If you already started in Microsoft Word, you can import a .docx file and continue working in Wordleaf.

Can I move a Google Docs book into Wordleaf?

Yes. Export the Google Docs document as a Word .docx file and import that file into Wordleaf. Wordleaf also accepts plain-text and Markdown files. This lets you continue a manuscript that began in Google Docs without copying every section by hand.

Exactly what files can Wordleaf import?

Editable writing imports currently include Microsoft Word .docx, Google Docs exported as .docx, plain text .txt, Markdown .md, native Wordleaf project files, and compatible project folders. Image assets can be added as PNG, JPG, JPEG, WebP, GIF, or SVG. Wordleaf does not currently advertise direct PDF-to-editable-document import, direct .doc export, direct .rtf import, or direct Google Docs cloud-account import.

Can Wordleaf import a PDF as editable writing?

Not currently. Wordleaf does not advertise direct PDF-to-editable-manuscript conversion. PDF files can exist as project assets in compatible project folders, but the normal editable-document import paths are Word .docx, Google Docs exported as .docx, plain text .txt, Markdown .md, native Wordleaf projects, and compatible project folders.

Can Wordleaf export a Word .docx file?

Not currently. The finished-document export is PDF, and the editable-project export is Wordleaf’s native project format. Plain-text or .docx export is not advertised until it exists as a finished export option.

What can Wordleaf export?

Wordleaf can export a finished PDF and can export the complete editable Wordleaf project so the manuscript, assets, settings, writing rules, and related project data can travel together and be reopened later. A separate plain-text export is not currently advertised.

Does Wordleaf keep backups?

Yes. Wordleaf supports local automatic manuscript backups every 15 minutes, every hour, or daily, plus manual backups and backup history. Backups can be restored from inside the program.

Publishing, PDFs, and page sizes
Exactly what page sizes can Wordleaf use?

Named formats currently include B1 (707 × 1000 mm), B2 (500 × 707 mm), B3 (353 × 500 mm), B4 (250 × 353 mm), A4 (210 × 297 mm), and US Letter (8.5 × 11 in). Book-oriented presets currently include 5 × 8 in, 6 × 9 in, 6 × 9 in with bleed, 8 × 10 in, and 8.5 × 11 in. The selected page profile is used by the live preview and PDF export.

Can Wordleaf make a publishing-ready PDF?

Yes. Wordleaf has a live PDF workflow and can export the current project as a finished PDF using the selected page profile. You can control page size and ask for document formatting such as margins, headers, footers, page numbers, spacing, headings, images, tables, figures, and other layout details before exporting.

Can I use the PDF for Amazon KDP or Kindle Direct Publishing?

Wordleaf can create a finished PDF for a book workflow, including projects intended for Amazon Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP) or other print and self-publishing services that accept PDF interiors. Wordleaf prepares the document; you still choose the publisher’s trim size, bleed, margins, cover requirements, metadata, and upload settings and should check the current requirements of the publishing service before submission.

How Wordleaf works
Does Wordleaf rewrite my writing automatically?

No. It is built to preserve your meaning, voice, terminology, intentional fragments, dialect, and style. It may clean speech-recognition artifacts or reorganize material when you ask, but substantive rewriting is supposed to happen only when you explicitly request it.

Will Wordleaf fill in missing story beats or blank outline sections for me?

No, not by default. “Organize” means arrange, group, label, map, or move your existing material. If a Hero’s Journey stage, 27-point beat, essay section, research section, chapter, or placeholder is empty, Wordleaf can identify the gap and help you think about it, but it leaves that missing content for you unless you ask it to help create something new.

Is Wordleaf an AI writer?

Wordleaf includes local language tools to understand dictation, context, organization, and document instructions, but its design is not “press a button and replace the author.” It is built to work with your words and your ideas, make the smallest requested change, and avoid silently filling in missing content.

Can Wordleaf help me figure out how to write a book?

It can help you take what you already know about the book and make it easier to work with: collect notes, dictate ideas, group related material, build and revise an outline, compare your material with common story structures, format chapters, add images, and prepare the final document. It will not decide what your book should be about or invent the missing book for you.

Is Wordleaf free?

Yes. Wordleaf is completely free to use. The Windows download is available now. A macOS build is planned and will be added when there is a real tested Mac release to download.
